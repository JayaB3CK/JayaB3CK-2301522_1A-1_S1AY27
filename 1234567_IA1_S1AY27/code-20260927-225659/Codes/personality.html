My Top Three Traits

1. The Logician (INTP-T)

I took the 16Personalities test and got INTP-T — the Logician. It means my brain is always running. I don't just accept things; I need to know why they work. In class, if something doesn't add up, I'll sit with it until it does. That's probably why I actually enjoy debugging code — it feels like solving a puzzle.

Impact: I understand concepts deeply instead of memorising them, which helps me in labs and exams where I have to explain my reasoning.

2. Creative (Always Making Something)

I've always been drawn to creative things — nails, painting, illustration. If I'm not doing coursework, I'm probably sketching or working on a set. It's how I reset. It also changes how I approach web design; I care about how things look and feel, not just whether they work.

Impact: My assignments usually have a personal touch because I treat them like mini-projects, not chores.

3. Introvert with a Social Battery

I'm the kind of person who needs to be "in the mood" to go out or even to talk. That doesn't mean I'm shy — it means socialising takes energy, and I have to manage it. I recharge alone, usually reading Asian illustrated comics or whatever novel I'm on that week.

Impact: I plan my week around my energy, not the other way around. On campus days I'm "on"; on other days I protect my quiet time so I can actually focus on work.

My Hero & My Motto

My Sister

My sister is my hero. I've watched her rebuild herself after every setback — jobs, relationships, health, all of it. She doesn't stay down. She takes the hit, gets back up, and figures out the next move. That's the kind of strength I want.

When I'm stressed about deadlines or feeling like I'm falling behind, I think about how she never uses one bad chapter as the whole story.

My Motto: "Burn the Boats"

"Burn the boats."

— Attributed to Hernán Cortés, 1519

In 1519, Cortés supposedly ordered his men to burn their ships when they landed in Mexico so there was no option to retreat — only forward. For me, that's what commitment looks like. No plan B. No half-committing to a goal and keeping an escape route open.

When I have a big assignment, I burn the boats. I clear my weekend. I tell people I'm unavailable. I put everything into it. It's terrifying, but it works.

Critical Essay: LLMs — Tool, Not a Crutch

When I first heard "LLMs must be used as a tool, not a crutch," I honestly felt seen. I've watched classmates paste entire assignments into ChatGPT and submit the output without even reading it. That's the crutch. But I've also used DeepSeek to check my code and point out mistakes I couldn't see — and that's a tool.

The difference is whether you're still doing the thinking. If I ask an LLM to "write my essay," I'm skipping the learning. If I ask it to "explain why this CSS selector isn't working," I'm using it to understand, and I still have to apply the fix myself. One builds skill; the other avoids it.

For me, LLMs are most useful in three situations: (1) debugging, when I need a second pair of eyes on code that won't run; (2) checking my grammar and clarity after I've written a draft; and (3) getting a plain-English explanation of a concept my textbook overcomplicates. In all three cases, I'm still the one doing the work — the LLM just makes the feedback loop faster.

The risk is real, though. Research from SAGE Open shows that students appreciate AI tools like DeepSeek for learning support, but the study also makes clear that the tool works best when students stay actively involved (Al-Obaydi & Pikhart, 2025). If you're passive, you learn nothing. If you're active, you learn faster.

My rule is simple: I use AI to ask better questions, not to avoid answering them. I read what it gives me, rewrite it in my own words, and test it myself. That's the line between a tool and a crutch.
